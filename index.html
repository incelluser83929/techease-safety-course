The problem

How people are being targeted online

Scammers increasingly target seniors and everyday internet users with tactics designed to create urgency, fear, or trust — then ask for money, passwords, or remote access to a device.

☎️

Tech support scams

A pop-up or phone call claims your computer is infected and asks you to pay for "help" or grant remote access.

✉️

Phishing emails & texts

Fake messages impersonate your bank, Amazon, or Social Security to trick you into clicking a link or sharing info.

👪

Grandparent & emergency scams

A caller pretends to be a family member in trouble, pressuring you to send money right away.

💻

Ransomware

Malicious software locks your files and demands payment to get them back — with no guarantee they will.

💞

Romance & trust scams

A stranger builds an online relationship over weeks or months, then asks for money for an "emergency."

🔑

Reused passwords

One password used everywhere means a single data breach can unlock many of your other accounts.

According to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3), adults aged 60+ reported billions of dollars in losses to online scams in a single year — and that's only the fraud that gets reported. Awareness is one of the strongest defenses we have.

How we calculate this

"Site visits since launch" is a real number, recorded by a small counter built into this site (one count per visitor session) — it is not simulated or borrowed from another site. The scams-avoided and dollars-protected figures are estimates built from that real number using two deliberately conservative, real-world-sourced assumptions:

Avoidance rate — 3%. Microsoft's Digital Defense Report found that basic security-awareness training on its own, without ongoing reinforcement, reduces phishing susceptibility by only about 3% — so we use that low, conservative figure rather than a more optimistic one (source).

Avoided loss — $500. The FBI's 2024 Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) report found an average reported loss of $19,372 per complaint overall, and $33,231 specifically for victims over 60 (source). Those averages are pulled way up by a relatively small number of very large-dollar cases — investment fraud, business email compromise — that a basic awareness course like this doesn't really address. So instead of using the full reported average, we use $500: a small, deliberately conservative stand-in for a typical loss from the more common scams this course covers, like phishing and tech-support scams.

The visit count is real; the scams-avoided and dollars-protected figures remain estimates built from it, not audited outcomes of this specific course.
